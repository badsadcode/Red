# OREGON LAKEHOUSE
## Atmospheric First-Person Wilderness Life & Underground Exploration Game

> **Working title:** Oregon Lakehouse (placeholder, not final)
> **Document type:** Concept and game-design brief for Sage
> **Status:** Brainstorming direction, not a locked specification
> **Genre:** First-person life sim / exploration / immersive survival-lite / sci-fi mystery
> **Core aesthetic:** PlayStation 1 geometry and texture sensibility, authentic retro screen effects, **modern lighting**

---

## 1. The Core Idea

You are on vacation in a secluded **lakeside log cabin somewhere in Oregon**, surrounded by an enormous, damp forest of tall trees and mountains. You live there day by day. Fish, forage, hunt, cook, take walks, chop wood, and enjoy the rain. Nothing necessarily needs to happen. The place should feel like somewhere you can simply **be**.

Then, on an otherwise ordinary day, you hear a thunderous noise. An unnatural **red light washes across the sky**, turning the clouds into a red sea. You investigate the apparent direction of the sound and discover a fenced-off entrance at the foot of a mountain: a sturdy metal door behind chain-link fencing, secured with a padlock. You break the padlock with your axe and enter.

Behind the door is **a gigantic, hidden research complex**, with the scale and exploratory possibilities of *Abiotic Factor* and *Half-Life*: vast halls, buried infrastructure, multiple interconnected sectors, strange experiments, abandoned workspaces, machinery, transport systems, and secrets far below the mountain.

**The game does not stop being a cozy wilderness game when this happens.** The cabin, lake, and forest remain your home, and the underground complex becomes an enormous second world you can choose to explore.

### One-sentence pitch

*A first-person game about living quietly in a lakeside Oregon cabin, until a red-sky event leads you to a colossal secret research facility buried beneath the mountains.*

### The central promise

**Some days you fish. Some days you descend into the impossible.**

## 2. Design Pillars

### 2.1 A place to live, not a list of chores

- The player should be happy to spend an evening at the cabin without advancing a quest.
- Routine activities must feel satisfying in their own right: fishing, preparing food, exploring trails, repairing things, sitting by a window during rain.
- Avoid aggressive survival meters, daily deadlines, constant enemy spawns, and mission-marker overload.
- Progress is available, but the world does not punish the player for choosing a quiet day.

### 2.2 Contrast is the game's identity

- **Above:** wet woods, still water, birds, wind, warm lamplight, a recognizable home.
- **Below:** industrial enormity, cold light, humming machinery, impossible architecture, distant metallic noises, uncertainty.
- A calm return home should feel almost as rewarding as a successful expedition.

### 2.3 Exploration over compulsory quests

- Curiosity drives the player: a distant structure, an unfamiliar path, a locked wing, a flickering light, a faraway machine sound.
- Navigation should reward observation, landmarks, maps, notes, shortcuts, and physical exploration.
- There can be objectives and discoveries, but avoid turning every activity into a checklist.

### 2.4 A truly immense, interconnected research complex

- Not a string of small generic rooms pretending to be a facility.
- Massive spaces, vertical depth, distinct departments, long-term secrets, transport infrastructure, and convincing connections.
- The player should frequently think: *How much of this place is still unexplored?*
- Controlled procedural systems may support scale, but recognizable layouts and good exploration must come first.

### 2.5 Retro visuals, contemporary atmosphere

- PS1-inspired models, low-resolution textures, restrained geometry, and appropriate post-processing.
- Pair with convincing dynamic illumination, shadows, weather, fog, and practical light sources.
- Keep effects adjustable so atmosphere does not damage readability or comfort.

## 3. Setting: The Surface World

### 3.1 Location and mood

A **fictional, remote Oregon lake valley**, surrounded by mountains and towering conifers. Think dense Douglas firs and other Pacific Northwest evergreens, mossy trunks, heavy understory, wet earth, fallen logs, boulders, and winding dirt paths.

**Weather:** overcast mornings, mist rolling across the lake, persistent drizzle, heavier rainfall, occasional storms, rare warm sunbreaks. The landscape is beautiful even when nothing is happening.

**Soundscape:** birds, wind through trees, rain on the roof, branches shifting, distant thunder, water lapping against shore, crackling stove, subtle creaks inside the house. Quiet is an active part of the design.

### 3.2 The summer house / cabin

The player's home is a **solid log cabin built from large timber logs**, immediately beside the lake. It should feel like an actual physical home, not a menu disguised as a building.

Potential features:

- Kitchen, stove, refrigerator or pantry, cooking equipment.
- Bed for sleeping and advancing time.
- Storage for food, tools, clothing, supplies, collected objects.
- Wood stove or fireplace for warmth and atmosphere.
- Porch overlooking the water; a place to sit during rain.
- Workbench for simple repairs and gear maintenance.
- Windows that show the actual outdoor world and weather.
- Optional future improvements and decorations without turning the game into a construction grind.

The cabin is a **stable, safe emotional anchor**. It should not be destroyed by unavoidable scripted events just to manufacture drama.

### 3.3 Surface activities

| Activity | Player interaction | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Fishing | Choose a spot, cast, wait, respond to bites, land fish | Relaxation, food, skill, weather-dependent discoveries |
| Hunting | Track wildlife, use appropriate tools, retrieve game | Food and meaningful wilderness interaction |
| Foraging | Search for mushrooms, berries, useful plants | Supports cooking and preparation, rewards observation |
| Cooking | Prepare simple meals from gathered ingredients | A satisfying ritual; supplies for expeditions |
| Walking and hiking | Follow or leave trails, explore the valley | Discovery and enjoyment of the environment |
| Wood gathering | Collect fallen wood / chop suitable logs | Cabin life, warmth, tactile activity |
| Canoeing / boating *(optional)* | Explore shorelines and inaccessible coves | Peaceful travel and new perspectives |
| Equipment upkeep | Repair basic tools, replenish lights and supplies | Gentle progression without tedious micromanagement |

**Important:** these activities should be usable **before and after** the facility is discovered. They are not a disposable tutorial.

### 3.4 The surface is not empty

Small points of interest can make the valley feel worth knowing: fishing spots, a derelict lookout, a creek crossing, a collapsed shed, old logging tracks, a cave, a high ridge, an isolated dock, abandoned service roads. Keep the sense of remoteness. Not every location must contain loot or a quest.

## 4. Opening and Inciting Event

### 4.1 First hours: ordinary life

The game opens at the cabin, already on vacation. Give the player genuine freedom to settle in.

- Wander around the house and lake.
- Learn simple interactions naturally.
- Fish, cook, gather supplies, explore nearby trails.
- Establish the familiar atmosphere and soundscape.

**Do not rush the mystery in the first five minutes.** Let the player develop a relationship with the cabin and valley.

### 4.2 The red sky

At some point after the initial quiet period:

1. A deep, startling sound travels across the valley.
2. A **red glow spreads across the sky**, staining the clouds and reflecting on the lake.
3. For a short time, the environment feels unnaturally wrong.
4. The player can see or infer the approximate direction of the disturbance: toward the foot of a mountain.
5. The world becomes quiet again, but not quite normal.

The event should feel impressive **without forcing the player immediately into a mission**. The player decides when to investigate.

**Design question (open):** Should the event happen after a flexible number of days, or be triggered by a natural exploratory milestone? The important thing is preserving genuine calm beforehand.

### 4.3 Finding the entrance

Following the direction of the sound, the player encounters:

- Old chain-link fence and warning signage.
- A neglected service road or utility structures.
- A **large, heavy metal door** set into the mountain.
- A padlocked access point.

The player **breaks the padlock with their axe**, opens the route, and steps inside. This is the first major threshold between the two identities of the game.

### 4.4 First descent

Start small and recognizable: a security vestibule, service corridor, perhaps a dead reception desk. Then reveal an interior space vastly larger than expected: a cavernous shaft, industrial hall, gigantic research atrium, or multi-level transportation hub.

The reveal should prompt one reaction: **This cannot possibly fit under that mountain.**

Do not immediately explain why it exists, what happened, or what the red sky meant. Let the place raise questions.

## 5. The Underground Research Complex

### 5.1 What it is

A vast, interlinked, partly abandoned and partly operational scientific / industrial megastructure. Its inspiration is the sense of scale, laboratories, systems, and discovery in **Half-Life** and **Abiotic Factor**, but it needs an original history, layout, identity, and mysteries.

The facility is **a second explorable world**, not a short linear dungeon.

### 5.2 Possible sectors (ideas, not mandatory canon)

| Sector | Character and gameplay possibilities |
|---|---|
| Access / Administration | Reception, security, offices, employee records, first clues |
| Service & Maintenance | Pipes, utility corridors, generators, access shafts, repairs |
| Research Laboratories | Experiments, sealed rooms, unusual equipment, environmental storytelling |
| Materials & Storage | Warehouses, logistics, crates, strange specimens, salvage |
| Power & Reactor | Immense turbines, substations, dangerous machinery, power routing |
| Residential Block | Cafeterias, dormitories, recreation, evidence of ordinary lives |
| Hydroponics / Biosciences | Overgrown chambers, cultivated ecosystems, biological anomalies |
| Flooded Levels | Pumps, rising water, alternate routes, underwater hazards |
| Internal Transit | Freight elevators, trams, maintenance trains, transport hubs |
| Restricted Experimental Wings | Unsettling environments, anomalous technology, unknown containment systems |
| Deep Infrastructure | Vast shafts and structures descending farther than expected |

Some spaces should be mundane and practical; others should be astonishing. Without the mundane, the extraordinary loses its effect.

### 5.3 Navigation and scale

- Build sectors with strong visual identities and memorable landmarks.
- Connect them through lifts, tram lines, service passages, stairwells, and maintenance corridors.
- Include shortcuts back to earlier sectors and, where logical, new exits to the surface.
- Make maps and environmental signage useful but imperfect.
- Support expeditions of different lengths: quick scavenging trips or deep multi-session journeys.
- Introduce safe rooms, rest points, caches, and optional underground outposts where they improve play.
- Avoid repetitive endless procedural corridors.

### 5.4 Interactions and systems

Possible systems for exploratory gameplay:

- Restore partial power to a wing.
- Restart pumps and access a flooded level.
- Repair or activate a freight lift or tram.
- Find access credentials, reroute doors, bypass damaged machinery.
- Bring useful tools back to the cabin.
- Gather supplies for longer descents.
- Discover documents, recordings, signs of former staff, or current inhabitants.
- Notice that strange activity in the complex sometimes affects the surface valley.

The goal is **physical problem-solving within a believable space**, rather than endless abstract keycards.

### 5.5 Danger and combat

The complex may contain hostile defense systems, escaped experiments, environmental hazards, unpredictable phenomena, and other threats. Their exact nature is deliberately undecided.

- Combat is real and can be exciting, but is **not required every few minutes**.
- Some corridors are safe, some are tense, some are dangerous.
- The player should be able to prepare and choose their risk level much of the time.
- Avoid trivializing the place by filling every room with monsters.
- Preserve moments of solitude, environmental storytelling, and awe.

This is **not automatically a full horror game**. It is an atmospheric life-and-exploration game with unsettling science-fiction mystery and occasional action.

## 6. The Daily Gameplay Loop

### Cozy day

1. Wake at the cabin to rain or morning light.
2. Make breakfast / check supplies.
3. Fish, forage, hunt, hike, paddle, repair something, or simply explore.
4. Cook dinner and relax at home.
5. Sleep; repeat.

### Expedition day

1. Wake at the cabin.
2. Prepare food, light sources, tools, protective gear, and a route.
3. Hike to a facility entrance or unlocked shortcut.
4. Explore a sector, solve a physical problem, survive hazards, discover clues or valuable materials.
5. Return to the cabin with supplies and questions.
6. Cook, organize, repair, plan the next outing.

### The desired rhythm

The player can freely alternate these. There should be **no artificial requirement to advance the mystery every day**.

> *Today I'm going fishing. Tomorrow I might go back to that locked laboratory on B-3.*

That's the feeling we are designing for.

## 7. Survival, Progression, and Inventory

### 7.1 Survival-lite, not punishment

Food, preparation, weather, and physical condition can matter, but not to the point where the player spends the whole game managing meters.

Recommended principles:

- Eating and cooking are useful, pleasant rituals, not frantic chores.
- Weather affects mood, visibility, travel and preparation.
- Long expeditions need planning, but the player can retreat and regroup.
- Avoid arbitrary timers and excessive resource drain.
- Make tools and supplies tactile and understandable.

**Open decision:** Whether hunger/thirst are explicit survival meters, softer stamina / well-being systems, or optional difficulty settings.

### 7.2 Equipment progression

The cabin phase begins with ordinary, plausible tools: axe, flashlight, fishing equipment, backpack, cooking gear, outdoor clothing, perhaps a hunting tool.

Exploration may unlock:

- Better lighting and batteries.
- Protective equipment and specialized tools.
- Additional storage and cooking options.
- Navigation or mapping aids.
- Equipment for flooded, contaminated, or electrically dangerous areas.
- Restored facility equipment and transportation shortcuts.

**Progress should increase possibilities, not eliminate peaceful routine.**

### 7.3 Home improvements

Possible light customization: organize shelves, improve the workshop, add storage, hang maps, display discoveries, improve warmth and lighting. Keep it intimate and home-like rather than forcing a giant base-building meta.

## 8. Art Direction

### 8.1 Technical visual target

**First-person perspective (FPP) throughout.**

- PS1-era-inspired **low-poly models** and chunky environmental geometry.
- Low-resolution pixelated textures with controlled filtering and pixel density.
- Retro texture shimmer / affine warping / vertex jitter where aesthetically useful.
- Optional low internal rendering resolution with pixel-perfect upscale.
- Dithering, limited-color feel, banding or subtle CRT / analog screen processing.
- **Modern dynamic lighting** with expressive shadowing, practical lights, rich day/night changes.
- Dense atmospheric fog and good weather effects, especially in the forest.
- Warm interior lighting in the cabin; cold institutional / emergency lighting below.

**Critical:** Do not make the picture ugly or unreadable just to imitate an old console. Retro shaders should be adjustable and, where possible, individually toggleable.

### 8.2 Surface palette and mood

- Deep wet greens, blue-gray cloud cover, dark bark, gray rock, pale lake mist.
- Warm gold-orange cabin lights contrasted against cool rainy daylight.
- Occasional sunlight breaking through trees, emphasizing scale.
- Red sky event should violently disrupt this familiar palette.

### 8.3 Facility palette and mood

- Aged concrete, industrial metal, exposed pipes, hazard markings.
- Fluorescent and industrial practical lighting; pockets of almost-total darkness.
- Emergency red, cold cyan, greenish institutional lights depending on the sector.
- Monumental scale conveyed through silhouettes, repetition, tiny walkways, and enormous machinery.

### 8.4 Audio is essential

- Surface: detailed rain layers, frogs/birds, wind, distant wildlife, footsteps on mud and wood, lake water, cabin stove.
- Underground: ventilation, electrical buzz, distant impacts, pipes, reverb, machinery, unnerving silence.
- Don't drown every scene in music. Natural and mechanical soundscapes should carry the atmosphere.
- The first red-sky noise should be memorable and physically enormous.

## 9. World and Storytelling Philosophy

**The player is an ordinary person on vacation.** They have no reason to assume a gigantic underground facility exists.

The red-sky event creates the mystery, but the truth should unfold through exploration, architecture, records, and consequences rather than immediate exposition.

Questions the game may raise:

- Who built the complex? When, and why?
- Why is it so huge?
- Is it genuinely abandoned?
- What caused the sound and red light?
- How much lies below the known levels?
- Has the facility affected the valley all along?
- Can the player safely leave the complex alone, or is something changing?

**Do not lock in answers yet.** Preserve mystery while early gameplay and environment design find their identity.

## 10. Suggested First Playable Slice for Sage

This is a **proposed sequence**, not a request to build the entire gigantic game immediately.

### Milestone A: A place worth staying

- One explorable lakeside cabin and short shoreline.
- Small forest area with giant trees, mountain silhouette, dense weather atmosphere.
- First-person controller and physical interaction.
- Day/night and convincing Oregon-style overcast/rain.
- One complete peaceful loop: catch a fish, cook it, sleep in the cabin.
- PS1-inspired graphics with modern lighting and adjustable retro post effects.

**Success test:** Is it pleasant to exist here for 20 minutes with no quest?

### Milestone B: The impossible interruption

- Distant sound event, dramatic red sky/cloud illumination, lake reflection.
- Navigable path toward mountain foot.
- Fenced entrance and metal door.
- Axe interaction that breaks the padlock.
- First interior approach and one spectacular large-scale reveal.

**Success test:** Does the contrast between familiar woods and impossible facility feel powerful?

### Milestone C: The first expedition loop

- A compact but coherent initial complex sector with at least one meaningful branch.
- Tool or environmental puzzle, interesting find, optional danger.
- Inventory, a useful item to retrieve, return path to the cabin.
- Persistent state across sleep/save/load.

**Success test:** Is returning home after exploring satisfying, and does the player want to go back?

### Milestone D: Establish depth

- Add verticality and a second distinct sector.
- Create a meaningful shortcut or transport connection.
- Introduce environmental storytelling and a longer-term mystery.
- Expand the surface with additional quiet activities and landmarks.

**Success test:** Does the complex feel like the beginning of something enormous rather than a sequence of rooms?

## 11. Things We Should Protect

1. **The cabin stays meaningful.** It is not thrown away once the facility opens.
2. **Quiet days are valid gameplay.** The player may choose to fish instead of investigating.
3. **The world feels physical.** Doors, paths, tools, food, and infrastructure are tangible.
4. **The complex feels huge and coherent.** Prioritize spatial logic and identity over empty procedural size.
5. **Danger has contrast.** Not every minute is combat; no constant scripted jump scares.
6. **The visual identity is intentional.** Real PS1 sensibility plus current-generation atmospheric lighting, not generic high-res realism with a grain filter.
7. **Mystery remains open.** Avoid prematurely deciding the entire backstory or ultimate ending.
8. **The design is expandable.** The first playable experience should be enjoyable even without every proposed sector or activity.

---

## 12. Open Creative Decisions

These are intentionally unresolved:

- Final title and the name of the lake / mountain.
- Exact in-world time period (modern, late 1990s, or deliberately ambiguous).
- Exact trigger and timing for the red-sky event.
- Whether the player can ignore the facility indefinitely.
- The nature and frequency of hostile threats.
- How explicit survival mechanics should be.
- Whether the surface is handcrafted, procedural, or hybrid.
- Whether the facility is wholly authored, modular/procedural, or hybrid.
- Whether underground travel eventually includes an operable train or tram.
- The true history of the facility, what the red event signifies, and whether anything is alive deep below.
- Optional future co-op or multiplayer; **not an assumed requirement**.

## 13. A Few Example Player Stories

**A rainy Tuesday:** I wake up, make breakfast, take my rod to the lake, catch trout, walk back as the rain starts, then cook and watch fog hide the opposite shore. I never even think about the mountain.

**The first discovery:** The sky turns red. A noise rolls across the valley like thunder from underground. Two days later I hike to the mountainside, break an old padlock, and realize the door opens into a place much too large to exist.

**A dangerous Thursday:** I pack food and batteries, head into an industrial maintenance sector, restore a lift, avoid something moving in the dark, and return exhausted. My cabin lights are visible through the trees. Home.

**A quiet Sunday after the mystery has begun:** I know a sealed research wing is waiting down there. But today I repair my dock, cook mushroom soup, and listen to the rain.

---

## Final Direction

We are not building a horror corridor with an outdoor tutorial. We are not building a survival grind with a sci-fi side quest. We are building **a place to live**, with a vast and mysterious second world hiding directly beneath it.

The defining moment isn't only opening the mountain door.

It's **coming home afterward**, standing on the cabin porch, listening to the rain, and realizing you can go back down tomorrow.

**Some days you fish. Some days you descend into the impossible.**
